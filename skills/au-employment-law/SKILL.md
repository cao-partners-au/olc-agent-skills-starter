# Australian Employment Law — Practitioner Reference

**Jurisdiction:** Federal (Commonwealth) + State overlay where noted
**Currency:** figures checked 12 November 2026 for the 2026-27 financial year. Thresholds and wages change every 1 July: check fairwork.gov.au and ato.gov.au before relying on any number.
**Not legal advice.** Use to reason through positions and draft language. Instruct a lawyer for high-stakes disputes.

## ⚡ Quick Answers — Most Common Questions

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who owns IP an employee creates at work? | The employer — Copyright Act 1968 s35(6) |
| Can an employee contract away IP ownership? | Yes, by agreement — s35(3) allows exclusion/modification |
| Is a restraint of trade clause enforceable? | Only if reasonable in scope, geography, and time — courts will blue-pencil or sever |
| What's the minimum notice period? | 1–5 weeks depending on service length (NES s117) — see table below |
| Can you dismiss someone on probation without notice? | No — NES minimum notice still applies unless serious misconduct |
| What's serious misconduct? | Theft, fraud, assault, refusing lawful direction, endangering safety — allows summary dismissal |
| When can an employee claim unfair dismissal? | After 6 months service (12 months small business), covered by award or earning under the high income threshold ($190,100 from 1 July 2026) |
| What's the super rate? | 12% (from 1 July 2025, unchanged for 2026-27). Since 1 July 2026 it must be paid with each pay run (Payday Super) |
| Is right to disconnect law? | Yes — from 26 August 2024 (15+ employees) and 26 August 2025 (small business) |
| Are non-competes banned? | Not yet. A draft bill (September 2026) would ban them for employees at or under the high income threshold, and ban non-solicitation clauses for everyone. Not law as at November 2026 |

## 1. Legislative Framework

### Primary Federal Legislation
- **Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth)** — cornerstone of Australian workplace law. Regulates minimum standards, employment terms, dispute resolution, unfair dismissal, general protections.
- **Fair Work Regulations 2009** — supporting rules on leave, record-keeping, payslips.
- **National Employment Standards (NES)** — 12 minimum entitlements that apply to all employees. Cannot be contracted out of.
- **Copyright Act 1968 (Cth)** — governs IP ownership in employment (see section 3 below).
- **Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992** — mandates employer super contributions.
- **Privacy Act 1988 (Cth)** — governs handling of employee personal information.

### Anti-Discrimination (Federal)
- Sex Discrimination Act 1984
- Racial Discrimination Act 1975
- Disability Discrimination Act 1992
- Age Discrimination Act 2004
- Anti-Discrimination and Human Rights Legislation Amendment (Respect at Work) Act 2022 — positive duty on employers to actively prevent sexual harassment

### Recent Reforms (Critical — check these)
- **Closing Loopholes Act 2023/2024** — strengthens sexual harassment protections, introduces right to disconnect, changes casual employee definitions
- **Payday Super** — in force from 1 July 2026: super must be paid with each pay run and reach the fund within 7 business days of payday. The ATO's Small Business Superannuation Clearing House closed on 1 July 2026
- **Non-compete ban (proposed, not yet law)** — exposure draft released September 2026: bans non-competes for employees at or under the high income threshold ($190,100), bans non-solicitation terms for all employees, and covers no-poach and wage-fixing arrangements. Intended to start in 2027; no start date legislated yet
- **Wage theft** — intentional underpayment has been a criminal offence since 1 January 2025
- **Paid Parental Leave** — government-funded PPL rose to 26 weeks from 1 July 2026, with super paid on it

## 2. National Employment Standards (NES)

The NES cannot be excluded by any contract, award, or agreement. They are the floor.

**The minimum entitlements:**
1. Maximum weekly hours (38 hours + reasonable additional)
2. Flexible working arrangement requests
3. Casual employees' right to change to permanent (the "employee choice" pathway since 26 August 2024)
4. Parental leave (up to 12 months unpaid, right to request 12 more)
5. Annual leave (4 weeks per year full-time; 5 weeks for shift workers)
6. Personal/carer's leave (10 days paid per year) + compassionate leave (2 days per event) + family and domestic violence leave (10 days paid)
7. Community service leave (unpaid, jury duty etc.)
8. Long service leave (state-based, varies)
9. Public holidays
10. Notice of termination and redundancy pay (see table below)
11. Fair Work Information Statement (must be given on commencement, plus the Casual Employment Information Statement for casuals)

Superannuation is not part of the NES; it comes from separate super guarantee law (section 7).

**Key rule:** An employment contract can give *more* than the NES, never less.

## 3. Intellectual Property Ownership

### The Default Rule — Employer Owns Work Created in Employment

**Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) s35(6):**
> Where a literary, dramatic, artistic, or musical work is made by an author in pursuance of the terms of his or her employment by another person under a contract of service or apprenticeship, that other person is the owner of any copyright subsisting in the work.

**Plain English:** If an employee creates something in the course of their employment, the employer owns the copyright. This is the legal default — not a negotiating position.

**"In pursuance of the terms of employment"** = created as part of the job. Courts look at:
- Was it within the employee's duties?
- Was it created during working hours or using employer resources?
- Was there a nexus between the work and the employment?

**s35(3):** The default can be modified or excluded by agreement. This is why employees try to contract around it.

### What Employees Legitimately Own

1. **Pre-existing IP** — anything created before the employment start date. The employer has no claim to this.
2. **Independent IP** — created outside employment, unrelated to the employer's business, not using employer resources or time. Genuinely the employee's.
3. **Underlying know-how** — skills, expertise, methodologies, general knowledge that exist in an employee's head. Not assignable as IP. An employee leaves with their brain intact — the employer cannot prevent them using general knowledge gained during employment (only specific confidential information is protectable).

### What Employers Legitimately Own

- All work created in the course of employment, related to the business
- Systems, code, automations, documents, processes built on the job using employer resources or time
- Anything within the scope of the employee's duties — even if not explicitly instructed

### Drafting Good IP Clauses

**Employer-protective IP clause (recommended structure):**
1. All work created in the course of employment and related to the business vests in the employer on creation
2. Employee assigns all right, title and interest — including moral rights (to the extent permitted)
3. Pre-existing IP explicitly excluded (list in schedule if needed for certainty)
4. Independent IP carved out — must be: unrelated to business, not on work devices, not using employer resources
5. Employee's general know-how and underlying expertise are not assigned (they can't be)
6. No separate licence required for deployment of work created in the role
7. Obligations survive termination

**Red flags in employee-proposed IP clauses:**
- Default presumption in favour of employee (inverts s35(6))
- Ownership tied to documented instructions or specific devices
- Requirement for separate licence to deploy work created in the role
- Overly broad definition of "pre-existing IP" that captures work clearly within the employment scope

## 4. Termination

### Minimum Notice Periods (NES s117)

| Years of continuous service | Minimum notice |
|---|---|
| Less than 1 year | 1 week |
| 1–3 years | 2 weeks |
| 3–5 years | 3 weeks |
| More than 5 years | 4 weeks |
| + 45 years old with 2+ years service | +1 week additional |

Notice must be given in writing. Employer can pay in lieu of notice (payment instead of working the notice period).

**Summary dismissal (no notice required):** Only for serious misconduct. Defined in Fair Work Regulations as:
- Wilful or deliberate behaviour inconsistent with continuing employment
- Conduct that causes serious and imminent risk to health/safety or reputation/viability of the business
- Examples: theft, fraud, assault, intoxication at work, refusing lawful and reasonable direction

### Unfair Dismissal

**Eligibility to claim:**
- Minimum employment period: 6 months (or 12 months for small business — fewer than 15 employees)
- Covered by an award or enterprise agreement, OR earning under the high income threshold ($190,100 from 1 July 2026)
- Compensation is capped at 26 weeks' pay, and no more than half the high income threshold ($95,050 for dismissals from 1 July 2026)
- Small businesses that follow the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code are treated as having dismissed fairly; keep the Code's checklist on file
- Must apply within 21 days of dismissal

**When dismissal is not unfair:**
- Valid reason (poor performance with prior warnings, genuine misconduct)
- Employee given opportunity to respond
- Genuine redundancy (job no longer required, proper consultation)

### Procedural Fairness (non-negotiable for performance dismissals)
1. Identify the issue
2. Notify employee in writing
3. Give opportunity to respond (with support person if requested)
4. Consider the response
5. Decide and notify

Skipping these steps exposes you to an unfair dismissal claim even if there is a valid reason.

### Redundancy Pay (NES)

| Years of service | Weeks of redundancy pay |
|---|---|
| 1–2 years | 4 weeks |
| 2–3 years | 6 weeks |
| 3–4 years | 7 weeks |
| 4–5 years | 8 weeks |
| 5–6 years | 10 weeks |
| 6–7 years | 11 weeks |
| 7–8 years | 13 weeks |
| 8–9 years | 14 weeks |
| 9–10 years | 16 weeks |
| 10+ years | 12 weeks |

Small businesses (fewer than 15 employees) are exempt from redundancy pay obligations.

## 5. Restraint of Trade

### Enforceability Test

Restraint clauses are presumptively void in Australia as contrary to public policy. To be enforceable, a restraint must:
1. Protect a legitimate business interest (confidential information, client relationships, trade secrets — not just competition)
2. Be reasonable in scope — what activities are restrained
3. Be reasonable in geography — where it applies
4. Be reasonable in duration — how long it lasts
5. Not be against the public interest

Courts do NOT enforce restraints simply because they were agreed. The reasonableness test is applied at the time of enforcement.

### Practical Guidelines

**Duration:** 6–12 months from termination is generally considered the outer limit. Beyond 12 months is very rarely upheld for employees (as opposed to business sale restraints).

**Geography:** Must match the employee's actual reach. National restraint for a state-based salesperson = unlikely to hold. National for a national-level executive = more defensible.

**Scope:** Must be targeted at the legitimate interest being protected. "Can't work in AI" = too broad. "Can't approach our current clients or use our confidential information in a competing AI recruitment business" = defensible.

### Cascading Clauses (best practice)

Draft restraints in cascading/waterfall form so courts can enforce the reasonable level:
```
The employee must not [activity] for:
(a) 12 months; or if not enforceable,
(b) 6 months; or if not enforceable,
(c) 3 months

within:
(a) Australia; or if not enforceable,
(b) Queensland; or if not enforceable,
(c) Brisbane metropolitan area
```
Courts apply the blue-pencil rule — sever unreasonable parts and enforce what remains. Cascading gives them a reasonable version to enforce.

### Non-Compete Reform (proposed, not yet law)

An exposure draft bill released in September 2026 would ban non-compete clauses for employees earning at or under the high income threshold ($190,100), ban non-solicitation clauses for all employees regardless of pay, and cover no-poach and wage-fixing arrangements between businesses. It is intended to start in 2027 but is not law as at November 2026. When drafting now: ask whether a restraint is truly needed, target it at senior or high-income roles, and rely more on confidentiality and IP clauses, which the draft does not ban.

## 6. Probation

**Key rules:**
- No special legal regime for probation in Australian federal law — it's a contractual concept
- NES notice periods still apply during and after probation — probation does not override minimum notice
- Unfair dismissal protections don't apply until minimum employment period (6 or 12 months)
- Probation period therefore works as a practical shield from unfair dismissal claims, not from notice requirements
- 6-month probation = standard. A longer probation does not change the unfair dismissal minimum employment period (6 months, or 12 months for small business)
- Performance management during probation still requires documentation and reasonable process to avoid general protections claims (adverse action)

## 7. Superannuation

- **Rate:** 12% (from 1 July 2025; unchanged for 2026-27)
- Contributions are mandatory — not negotiable
- **Payday Super (from 1 July 2026):** super must be paid with every pay run and reach the employee's fund within 7 business days of payday (limited exceptions, such as a new employee's first pay). Quarterly payment is no longer allowed. Late payment attracts the super guarantee charge. The ATO's Small Business Superannuation Clearing House closed on 1 July 2026, so use your payroll system or another clearing service
- Super is calculated on Ordinary Time Earnings (OTE) — base salary, not overtime unless contract specifies otherwise
- Maximum super contribution base for 2026-27: $270,830 a year
- Salary sacrifice arrangements can affect super calculations — document clearly in contracts

## 8. Confidentiality

### What is Protected

- Genuine trade secrets: protectable indefinitely, survives employment (even without a clause)
- Confidential information: protected during employment as implied duty; post-employment requires express clause
- General skills and knowledge: NOT protectable — employee takes their brain with them

### Drafting Rules
- Define confidential information specifically — generic "all information" clauses are harder to enforce
- Include a carve-out for publicly available information and general knowledge
- Post-employment obligations must be reasonable in duration (typically 12–24 months after termination for non-trade-secret confidential info)
- AI and cloud services consideration: data submitted to third-party AI models constitutes disclosure to a third party — employers should have appropriate data processing agreements in place before directing employees to use AI tools on confidential data

## 9. Workplace Health & Safety

- **Work Health and Safety Act 2011 (Cth)** — primary duty of care on employers (PCBUs) to ensure health and safety of workers so far as reasonably practicable
- Applies to physical and psychosocial hazards (bullying, harassment, stress, workload)
- Remote/work-from-home workers are covered
- Officers of a company have personal due diligence obligations — not just the company
- AI-heavy workplaces: consider psychosocial risk from high-pressure automated systems, 24/7 monitoring, and performance tracking — these are real WHS exposure areas
